Ethan Mollick made an interesting observation about [coding with AI assistants](https://x.com/emollick/status/2007249835465072857): the skills that make you effective at directing coding agents are basically management skills. Clear goal-setting. Providing context. Breaking down tasks. Giving feedback.

This made me laugh because I spent years thinking I'd chosen between "technical work" and "management work." Turns out they might have been the same skill set all along, just pointed at different collaborators.

When I work with Claude Code, I'm not really writing much code anymore. I'm doing something else:

- Articulating what I want clearly (like explaining requirements to a new team member)
- Providing relevant context about the existing codebase (like onboarding someone)
- Breaking large features into smaller, manageable pieces (like sprint planning)
- Reviewing the output and giving constructive feedback (like... code review)

The difference is scale and speed. Where I might have a handful of one-on-ones per week, I'm having hundreds of these micro-interactions with AI. And the feedback loop is instant.

But here's what surprised me: I'm not sure which direction the skill transfer actually goes. Did years of programming make me better at management? Or would someone who came up through people management be just as effective at directing AI coding assistants?

[Simon Willison notes](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jan/4/coding-again/) that people who haven't coded in years—maybe they moved into management, or got busy with kids, or just let their skills get rusty—are suddenly productive again. They still have the high-level thinking. They know what good software looks like. They can describe what they want. The AI handles the syntax.

This feels like it might be democratizing in an interesting way. Not "everyone can code now" (that always felt hollow), but "people who understand systems and can communicate clearly can direct the building of software."

I spent a decade learning to think in code. Now I'm realizing I was also learning to decompose problems, provide clear direction, and iterate based on feedback. The code was just the medium.

Maybe the real skill was always the thinking. The code was just how we used to have to express it.
